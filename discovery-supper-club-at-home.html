A PlotNua Discovery

What if your home could become someone else’s favourite restaurant? The Supper Club at Home

In a handful of Irish homes, chefs already cook for small groups of paying guests: one long table, a set menu, strangers who leave as friends. Drag the handle to see the same room, set for the evening.

The possibility

The table is already there.

Most homes have a room that comes alive a few times a year: Christmas, a birthday, a long Sunday lunch. The rest of the time, the table waits.

Chefs, meanwhile, have the menus, the skills and often a following, but not always a room. Restaurants are expensive to open. Pop-up venues are hard to find and rarely feel like anywhere.

A home feels like somewhere. That’s what guests at a supper club are paying for: the food, and the feeling of being welcomed into a real place, by real people, for one evening only.

01 · Same room. Different evening.

Nothing here needs a renovation.

Same room, same table, same light, set for a chef and a table of guests for the night.

  • The everyday things come off the table; linen, plates, candles and flowers go on.
  • The lights come down. The kitchen island becomes the pass.
  • A chef cooks and serves. Guests who booked a seat arrive at the front door.

02 · It’s already happening in Ireland

Irish tables, already set.

Home supper clubs are rare in Ireland, but they exist. Most begin the same way: a cook opens their own home to a small group of paying guests.

Dublin 7 · reported in The Irish Times

A house in Dublin

Two nights a month, one table

A chef and her co-host run a supper club two nights a month in the co-host’s own home: a six-course set menu, tickets bought in advance, and guests, many of whom come alone, seated together around one table.

Irish home supper clubs will be profiled here only with each club’s agreement. Each of these evenings began at the host’s own table. The next step, a chef cooking in a home that isn’t their own, is the idea this Discovery explores. PlotNua isn’t connected with any supper club.

03 · Three operating models

Three ways to set the table.

Every home supper club is a mix of three things: a cook, a room and guests. Who brings which, and who sells the tickets, gives three different shapes.

2 The borrowed room

You have the room. A chef has the menu.

You make your dining room, kitchen, conservatory or garden available for an evening. A professional chef plans the menu, cooks, serves and sells the tickets. Guests arrive at your door as diners, and leave having eaten somewhere they could never have booked.

You might host on the night, or simply hand over the table. You and the chef agree the arrangement between you: a fee for the room, a share of the evening, or simply a seat at your own table.

An idea to explore. We haven’t yet found a verified example of a chef running ticketed dinners in someone else’s home in Ireland, so treat this as a possibility, not a proven model.

1

The chef’s own table

A chef hosts paying guests at their own table. It’s how Irish home supper clubs begin.

3

The homeowner’s evening

You plan a ticketed evening (a harvest dinner, a fundraiser, a long table for the neighbourhood) and engage a chef to cook. It gives you the most control, and the most to organise: you’d be closer to running the event yourself.

Not a dinner party. Cooking for friends who don’t pay is something else entirely. This Discovery is about evenings where guests buy tickets.

04 · The homeowner–chef partnership

Who brings what?

A good evening is a partnership. Here’s how the borrowed room usually divides up.

The homeowner
  • A room or garden that seats the table
  • A kitchen the chef can work in, or space to set up
  • A guest toilet, and a clear way in and out
  • A welcome at the door, if you choose to host
  • Telling your insurer, and checking the practical side
The chef
  • The menu, the cooking and the service
  • Ingredients, equipment and any extra hands
  • Food safety and written allergen information
  • Selling the tickets and looking after guests
  • Their own registration and insurance
The guests
  • A ticket bought in advance
  • An evening at a table they couldn’t book anywhere else
  • Their dietary needs, shared when they book
  • Respect for the home they’re in
How an arrangement might work
  1. Meet first. Talk through the room, the kitchen, the numbers and the date. Walk the chef through the house.
  2. Agree the basics in writing. Guest numbers, times, what’s included, who cleans, what happens if the evening is cancelled, and how you’ll each be paid or rewarded.
  3. Check the practical side together (see Before the first ticket).
  4. Start with one evening. Then decide whether to do it again.

05 · Property suitability

What makes a room work?

The best supper club rooms aren’t the grandest. They’re the ones where a long table fits comfortably, the chef can move, and guests can come and go without squeezing past the cooker.

  • 01Room for the table without blocking a door or a way out
  • 02A kitchen the chef can use, or a corner to set up
  • 03A guest toilet that doesn’t mean walking through bedrooms
  • 04A clear, well-lit route in, and a clear way out
  • 05The neighbours: parking, arrivals and late finishes
  • 06Your home’s own rules: landlord, management company, mortgage
  • 07For gardens: cover, lighting, level ground and an Irish-weather plan
  • 08Step-free access, if you can offer it

Practical requirements

Before the first ticket.

Selling tickets turns a dinner into something more formal. Being occasional or small doesn’t automatically put it outside the rules. These are the things to check, ideally before you set a date. We’re confirming several details with the relevant Irish authorities and will update this section.

Food registration

Register before you start

In Ireland, a food business has to register before it starts, even when it operates from home.

Still being confirmed: whether the chef, the homeowner or both need to register, and whether each host home must be notified.

Allergens

Allergens, in writing

Guests need to be told, in writing, about allergens in what they’re served.

Still being confirmed: exactly what applies when tickets for a set menu are sold in advance.

Planning

The use of the house

Regularly using a house for paying guests can raise a planning question about the use of the property. Your local authority’s planning department can advise, and you can ask it formally whether something needs permission.

Still being confirmed with the planning authority.

Fire safety

Exits, alarms and numbers

When the public comes into a building, the person in control of it has fire-safety duties. Think about exits, alarms, candles and how many people the room can safely hold.

Still being confirmed with the fire authority.

Insurance

Tell your insurer first

Tell your home insurer before you host. Many home policies treat paying guests or business use differently. The chef should have their own public and product liability cover.

Still being confirmed with an insurance broker.

Drinks

Alcohol needs advice

Selling alcohol needs a licence. Leaving drinks out of the ticket price doesn’t settle every licensing question: whether guests can bring their own, and whether anything can be charged for it, are still open. Take advice before offering drinks in any form.

Open question: professional advice needed.

TaxIncome from tickets or a room fee

Income from tickets, or a fee for your room, may be taxable. Revenue’s guidance, or an accountant, will tell you how.

Your home’s rulesLandlords, apartments and mortgages

Tenants need their landlord’s agreement; apartments may have management-company rules; check your mortgage terms.

None of this is meant to put you off. Home supper clubs run successfully in Ireland. It’s simply easier to set up properly from the start. This page is general information, not legal, tax or insurance advice. Last reviewed 27 October 2026.

Commercial potential

What a table could mean.

For a chef, a beautiful room can be the difference between an idea and a sold-out night.

For a homeowner, it might mean a fee for the room, a share of the evening, or simply the best seat at a table you’d never have set yourself. Some hosts do it for the people they meet.

What works depends on the numbers at the table, the menu and the price, and on what you agree together. There’s no standard rate, and we won’t pretend there is. Talk it through openly, agree it in writing, and start with one evening.
