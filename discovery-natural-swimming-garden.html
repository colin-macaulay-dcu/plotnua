The Natural Swimming Garden

What if you could go wild swimming without leaving home?

A natural swimming pond is a carefully designed part of the garden, with plants and biological filtration helping to maintain the water without conventional chlorine treatment.

Illustrative AI-generated visualisation. Not an actual installation.

The possibility

Your own swim, at the end of the garden.

Picture a summer evening. You walk down the garden, past the planting, and step into clear water edged with reeds and irises. No changing rooms, no car park, no journey to the coast. Just your own garden.

It isn’t a fantasy. Natural swimming pools have been designed and built in private gardens in Ireland, including in Co. Louth, north Cork and Co. Laois.

01 · From lawn to swimming pond

It starts with the garden you already have.

An open lawn, planting at the edges, and room to think differently about both.

02 · It’s already happening in Ireland

Three Irish gardens built around the water.

In Ireland it’s a bespoke landscape project, usually designed by a landscape architect or garden designer and built by a landscape contractor.

Co. Louth

Near Carlingford

A garden built around the water

Near Carlingford, a contemporary garden was created around a natural swimming pool. The garden was designed by Andrew Dunne of Andrew Christopher Garden Design, and built by Silverstream Landscapes.

In 2020 an Irish natural swimming pool garden won an award at the ALCI Landscape Awards: this project earned Silverstream Landscapes the ALCI’s Landscape Contractor of the Year award. The designer describes the project as almost two years in the making, including a detailed planning application.

North Cork

Filled by the rain

A pond fed from the roofs

A natural swimming pool in rural north Cork features in the portfolio of Cathal O’Meara Landscape Architects. Its water is harvested from the roofs of nearby outbuildings. A shallow planted zone helps clean the water and is warmed by the sun. A second natural pool in the practice’s portfolio is fed from a well on the site.

Co. Laois

A family’s own pond

A self-built family pool

Near Durrow, artist and permaculturist Marc Rome built a natural pool for his own family, as reported by The Irish Times in 2022. It’s about 16 metres by 6 metres and holds around 117,000 litres, with a reed bed and an iris zone working alongside a simple garden pump.

Projects are described from published sources. Their inclusion doesn’t mean the designers or builders currently offer natural swimming pools, or that they are connected with PlotNua. No project photographs are shown.

03 · What a natural swimming pond actually is

Designed for swimming from the start.

A natural swimming pool is cleaned by biological filtration (plants, a biological filter, or both) instead of chlorine.

It isn’t an ordinary garden pond that someone decided to swim in. Most have two connected parts: a deeper area for swimming, and a shallower, planted area where the water is cleaned. Many also use a pump and filter to keep the water moving.

That’s what makes it different from a pond made for fish or for looking at: its depth, its filtration and the way the water is managed are all planned around people swimming in it. It still needs looking after. Plants are cut back, debris is cleared, and the water is checked through the season.

Accessible versionRead the diagram as text

How a natural swimming garden works. A natural swimming pond uses plants, natural microorganisms and careful design to help keep the water clean, clear and healthy without conventional chlorine treatment.

1 · Water flows naturally. Water moves between the swimming zone and the planted regeneration zone, either naturally or with a small pump depending on the design.

2 · Plants and microorganisms clean the water. Aquatic and marginal plants take up excess nutrients, while beneficial bacteria living on plant roots and in gravel help break them down.

3 · Clean, clear water for swimming. The result is clean, clear water in the swimming zone, without the need for conventional chlorine or chemical treatment.

Planted regeneration zone: a shallow, planted area where nature does the work. Swimming zone: a deeper area for swimming, typically 1.5–2.0 metres.

Three ways to bring natural swimming into your garden. Designs vary in appearance, size, maintenance and cost. The three common approaches are described in section 04 below.

Important: natural swimming ponds can be designed to work with nature, without conventional chlorine treatment. Maintenance requirements vary between designs, and all systems require some care. The exact design, cost and ongoing maintenance depend on the site, the installer and your individual circumstances.
04 · Three natural swimming approaches

Three ways to bring natural swimming into a garden.

They differ in how much the planting does, how much equipment is involved and how the water looks. The first is the one this Discovery is about.

1 The primary approach

Plant-led natural swimming pond

Plants, microorganisms and planted regeneration areas provide biological filtration. Some designs use little mechanical equipment.

  • A planted regeneration area sits beside the swimming water and does much of the cleaning.
  • Some plant-led designs can have relatively modest maintenance needs: seasonal plant care, clearing debris and regular checks on the water.
  • It needs the most room of the three, which is why it suits a generous garden.
2

Hybrid natural swimming pond

Combines planted regeneration areas with pumps or additional biological filtration.

  • The north Cork and Co. Laois examples both combine planting with a pump.
  • Care includes the planting and routine checks on any equipment.
3

Compact biologically filtered pool

Uses engineered biological filtration, sometimes with little visible aquatic planting. Compact, biologically filtered pools exist elsewhere in Europe, from about 25 m² of water. They look more like a conventional pool than a pond, and we haven’t found an installer offering them in Ireland.

Current installation availability in the Republic of Ireland has not been verified.

All three rely on biological filtration rather than conventional chlorine treatment. Maintenance varies between designs, and every one still needs some care and regular checks on the water.

An idea for a generous garden.

So the first question isn’t really “could I have one?” It’s “where would it go?” Think about:

  1. the flattest, sunniest part of the garden;
  2. how a digger would get in;
  3. where the water will come from;
  4. how the pond will sit beside everything else you use the garden for.
Before you go further

The practical side, early.

Beyond the pond itself, a project may involve excavation, a liner, filtration equipment, pumps, planting and landscaping. These are the practical considerations worth understanding early.

Planning

Expect a planning conversation

A natural swimming pond should be treated as a project likely to require planning permission. The planning exemptions for ordinary garden ponds should not be assumed to apply to swimming ponds. Check with your local planning authority before proceeding. A Section 5 declaration can provide a formal determination on whether proposed works are exempt.

Children

Supervision comes first

Children can drown in a few centimetres of water. Fences and covers reduce the risk but are no substitute for supervision.

Water quality

Clear water still needs checking

Ireland does not have a specific water-quality standard for private natural swimming pools. Biological filtration still requires maintenance and water-quality monitoring. Owners should agree a suitable testing and maintenance programme with their pool designer and an appropriate testing provider.

Water supply and drainage

A lot of water, planned for

Natural swimming ponds can require substantial quantities of water. One documented Irish family pool holds approximately 117,000 litres. Restrictions on public water use can affect filling pools and ponds. Water supply, overflow and any eventual emptying should be considered during design and agreed with the relevant authorities or service providers where required.

Electrics

Pumps and lighting

Where pumps, filtration equipment or lighting require electrical installation work, use a Registered Electrical Contractor and obtain the appropriate completion certification.

Insurance

Tell your insurer

Tell your home insurer before you build one, and check how your policy treats it.

Garden sizeIs there room?

Planted designs need space: about 40 m² of pond for around 20 m² of swimming water, before paths and terraces. Traditional planted swimming ponds generally suit larger gardens; requirements vary with the design.

Ground conditionsWhat lies beneath the lawn

The ground shapes the design. The award-winning Co. Louth pool was built on a site with a very high water table. A designer will want to understand your soil, levels and water table before anything is dug.

Excavation accessHow a digger would get in

A swimming pond means excavation, and machinery has to reach the garden. Side access, gates and the route through the garden all matter, and so does where the excavated soil will go.

MaintenanceWhat looking after it involves

Plants are cut back, debris is cleared, and the water is checked through the season. Some plant-led designs can have relatively modest maintenance needs, but every design needs some care, and any pumps or filters need routine checks.

What does it cost?

A substantial landscape investment.

We haven’t found published Irish prices for natural swimming pools. Costs depend on the site and design, so ask for a quote.

We’d rather tell you that than print a figure we can’t stand behind. What we can say is that a natural swimming pond is a substantial landscape investment. A designer can help you understand what your garden would involve before anything is dug.

Your next step

Start with your garden, not with a pool.

Talk to a landscape architect or garden designer about your garden first. It helps to bring:

  • a rough plan or photos of the garden, and any slopes or levels;
  • the space you think you could give to the pond and its surroundings;
  • where water would come from: mains, a well or collected rainwater;
  • who will use it, including any young children;
  • anything you’ve already heard from your local planning authority.
