The Underground Swimming Pool

What if the swimming pool you’ve always wanted was hiding beneath your garden?

Look closely at the lawn. That strip of glass is the only clue.

Illustrative AI-generated visualisation. Not an actual installation.

What if there were an entire swimming pool beneath your garden?

It is ambitious, expensive and architecturally complex. But pools like this have been built beneath gardens, with the lawn put back on top. Here’s what it would really take.

01 · What lies beneath

The rooflight is only a window.

From the garden you see a strip of glass. The pool room beneath it is far larger: it runs on under the lawn, with soil, a structural roof and the garden itself on top.

02 · How it could be built

Dug out, built as a box, then hidden again.

An underground pool is a basement project first and a swimming pool second. In outline, a project like this would usually go through five stages.

An illustration of the idea, not an engineering drawing or a construction method. Every real project is designed by its own architect and engineer for its own site.

  1. 01

    Survey and design

    Ground conditions, groundwater, drainage, access for machinery and the neighbouring buildings are investigated before anything is drawn.

  2. 02

    Permissions

    Planning permission and the building control process come before any digging (see below).

  3. 03

    Retaining walls, then excavation

    The walls that hold back the ground are usually formed first, and the soil inside them is then dug out. At one London project the excavation was made inside a wall of concrete piles cast in 16-metre holes.

  4. 04

    The structure

    A waterproofed concrete box is built: floor, walls, the pool tank and a structural roof strong enough to carry soil and garden.

  5. 05

    The garden returns

    Soil goes back over the roof, the lawn and planting are reinstated, and only the rooflight and a discreet vent remain visible.

03 · The practical side

Why it isn’t for every garden.

Most of the difficulty is invisible once it’s finished. These are the questions a design team would work through first.

Ground and structure

Holding back the ground

Digging several metres down beside boundary walls and neighbouring houses needs engineered retaining walls and a careful plan for keeping the excavation stable while the work goes on.

Groundwater

Water from outside, too

A room below ground sits in the groundwater. The design has to keep water out, and to understand how groundwater moves around the new structure so that neighbouring properties aren’t affected.

Waterproofing and drainage

A dry box below a wet garden

The chamber needs a complete waterproofing strategy, and rainwater landing on the rooflight and the garden above needs somewhere to go. Pumps and sumps are common below ground.

Air

Humidity, ventilation and heat

A heated indoor pool fills a room with warm, humid air. Mechanical ventilation and dehumidification are essential, not optional, and the plant needs its own space and an air path to the outside.

Radon

Below-ground rooms and radon

Radon is a natural radioactive gas from the ground. Building regulations in Ireland include radon provisions for new buildings, and a room below ground deserves particular attention in the design.

Safety and access

Getting in, getting out, and children

The room needs a safe way in and out, and a pool needs constant supervision of children. Lockable doors and pool covers reduce risk; they don’t replace supervision.

MaintenanceWhat looking after it involves

An indoor pool runs every day: water treatment, filtration, heating and the air-handling plant all need routine servicing, and the waterproofing and drainage need checking over the life of the building.

ConstructionLiving beside the works

Excavation means machinery, lorries removing soil and months of work in the garden. Access, where the soil goes, noise and the neighbours all shape the plan.

04 · Planning in Ireland

Expect to need planning permission.

An underground swimming pool should be treated as a project likely to require planning permission.

The planning exemptions for house extensions and for sheds and similar garden structures do not mention excavated swimming pools, so they should not be assumed to apply. Speak to your local planning authority early. A Section 5 declaration can give a formal answer on whether specific works are exempt.

An underground pool room connected to a house is likely to be treated as an extension. Since 27 July 2026, extensions to a dwelling over 45 square metres come within the building control commencement and certification process, so ask your architect how it applies to your project.

Planning decisions turn on the site. In 2025, permission for a basement-level pool-house extension in Howth, Co. Dublin, was refused on appeal; the reasons concerned the visual impact in a protected landscape area and a separate enforcement matter, not the idea of building below ground.

05 · What does it cost?

Among the largest projects a home can take on.

We haven’t found published Irish prices for an underground pool.

It combines a basement build, a swimming pool and a climate-controlled room, so it is one of the most substantial projects a homeowner can undertake. Costs depend heavily on ground conditions, access, size and finish. An architect and a structural engineer can tell you what your garden would involve before anything is designed in detail.

06 · It has been done

Real underground pools.

Private pools below ground exist in Irish homes. Pools built beneath a garden and covered over again are rarer: the clearest published examples are in London.

In Irish homes, below the house

Limerick · Completed 2008

A spa beneath a family home

Carmody Groarke renovated a 19th-century mansion and added a network of underground rooms and a subterranean spa beneath the family home. Its 17-metre pool is lined in black basaltina stone.

Architect: Carmody Groarke

Dublin 4

Shrewsbury Road

A new seven-bedroom Victorian-style house in a conservation area includes a basement leisure suite with a swimming pool, Jacuzzi, gym and steam room.

Initial design concept: the late Sam Stephenson · Planning design and project management: Nicholas Sweeney Architects

In London, beneath a reinstated garden

Holland Park, London · Completed

A pool with a moving floor

A moving-floor swimming pool was inserted beneath the rear garden of a Grade II* listed house, and the garden was reinstated in a simple formal style. Rooflights and a curved lightwell bring daylight below, and the floor can rise so the room doubles as a games room or cinema.

Architect: Haines Phillips Architects

Explore this project → (opens the architect’s project page in a new tab)
Wimbledon, London

Twenty metres beneath the garden

A 20-metre pool and a gym sit in a new basement now entirely covered by the garden, built inside a wall of concrete piles cast in 16-metre holes. Rooflights in the gym double as sections of glass floor in the garden above.

Architect: Dyer Grimes Architects

Your next step

Start with your site, not with the pool.

The first question is whether your ground, your boundaries and your budget could support a structure like this at all.

Step 1

An architect

A residential architect can tell you whether the idea suits your site, and lead the design and the planning application.

A structural engineer
