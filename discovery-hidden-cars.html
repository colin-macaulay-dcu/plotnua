Hidden Cars

Two cars. One parking space.

What if they could both fit?

The Opportunity

What if your driveway could hold more?

When another car arrives, finding room for it usually means finding more ground. Some parking systems offer another possibility.

The same space, twice
  • One spaceOne car.What the parking space does today, and the limit almost every driveway runs into.
  • The same spaceTwo cars.What it could do, without taking another metre of ground from the property.
How the same space holds two
  • Some systems stack one car above another. Others lower a car below the driveway.
  • Actual capacity depends on the system and the property. A specialist survey is needed to know what could work at yours.
Where the cars could go
  • Parking stackers One car rests on a platform above another. The outdoor type used at houses stands about a metre and a half above ground — lower than many garden walls — because most of it sits in a shallow pit. It asks least of the site, and it is the one option that still looks like machinery.
  • Concealed parking lifts The car is lowered a short distance and the surface closes over it, so the approach reads as paving. The dig is shallower than a full chamber. Where a stacker adds a space, a concealed lift removes a car from the view.
  • Underground garages A platform carries the car into a sealed room beneath the property and the ground closes above it. The most complete answer and by a distance the most demanding. It returns the surface entirely, and it is a construction project.
  • Automated systems You leave the car on a platform and the system moves, stores and returns it. For several cars in a footprint that will never hold them side by side. Long established in European buildings, now specified at house scale, with the servicing to match.

Planning, ground and the professionals
The exemption, and why it runs out. The exemption covering car parking spaces and hard surfaces applies to a hard surface to the front or side of the house, for parking no more than two cars, and only where ground level changes by no more than one metre. A lift pit is typically two to three metres, so the exemption is exceeded several times over before anything else is considered — and a lift is machinery, not a surface, so the exemption does not extend to it. The common outdoor stacker sits in a pit deep enough to break the same one-metre limit; surface-mounted versions avoid the pit, but the exemption still covers a hard surface for parking, not a machine placed on it. A Section 5 Declaration from your local authority gives a formal determination for your specific proposal.

Your development plan carries as much weight as the regulations. Dublin City, for example, requires a Basement Impact Assessment with every application including a basement, and a ground investigation report as part of it. It does not in general support basements close to protected structures, applies a general presumption against basements in Zones of Archaeological Interest, and applies a general restriction against basements below the estimated flood levels for Flood Zones A or B. It also expects a domestic basement to stay within the footprint of the original house, no deeper than one storey, and generally over no more than half the garden. Other authorities set their own — check the plan that governs your address, not Dublin’s.

Groundwater. A chamber below ground in wet Irish soil needs tanking, drainage and sometimes permanent pumping. Where the winter table sits close to the surface the engineering escalates sharply. A ground investigation answers this, not an opinion.

A neighbour’s foundations are closer than they look. On terraced and semi-detached houses the dig happens within metres of the house next door. That is a professional question and a neighbour question at the same time.

Water that used to soak away needs somewhere to go. Replacing permeable ground with a sealed chamber changes how the site drains.

Who you would need. An architect or planning consultant, a structural engineer, drainage input, and a specialist installer. Building Regulations apply whether or not planning permission is needed, and ESB Networks must be contacted before any work near overhead lines — the minimum clearance is six metres for lines up to 38kV and ten metres at 110kV and above.

What it costs, and why nobody will quote you yet
No Irish supplier publishes prices, and we are not going to invent a range. Published international figures for lift units alone have run from roughly $12,000 to $60,000 and above depending on type, and one reported UK quotation came to £33,000 plus VAT plus groundworks — and that last phrase is where the real number lives. Both are indicative, neither is Irish, and neither is recent enough to quote at a supplier. Every Irish installer quotes after a survey.

Equipment prices are broadly knowable. Digging, structure, waterproofing and drainage are specific to your site and are frequently the larger number. Any quote for a lift on its own is a fraction of a total.

Two things make the numbers meaningfully better. A sloping site, where a lift or garage can be driven into a level change that already exists instead of dug out of flat ground; and work already underway, where plant and access are being paid for anyway.

It tends to make sense where the frontage, the architecture or the cars themselves are worth protecting. The engineering is the same wherever it goes, and the sites that need it most are tight urban and suburban ones where a conventional garage is impossible — but the excavation alone puts it beyond most domestic budgets.

PlotNua does not sell or install these systems. What we’re building is a way to help you understand whether one could work at your property — and, when that part is ready, connect you with the specialists who can take it further.

Living with it, and will the car even fit
Measure the car before pricing the system. Specifications differ sharply, so check against the model rather than the category. Capacity is commonly around 2,000kg per space and lengths sit near five metres, but height limits range from about 1.5m on some systems to over two metres on others. Many SUVs are near the ceiling on the generous ones and beyond it on the rest, most vans exceed it, and a large electric car can approach the weight limit.

Day to day it is a machine. It needs power, it needs servicing on a schedule, and it needs a plan for the day it stops with a car inside. Systems are specified with manual or backup release and servicing contracts exist for this, but it has to be designed in and asked about before purchase. Ask any supplier directly what happens on power loss.

The surface above. The platform closes flush and the surface can be finished to match its surroundings, including as parking for another car. What that surface can carry, and how planting works over a sealed structure, are design questions to settle early rather than assume.

Ask what they have actually built. The technology is mature; the Irish experience of it is not. Very few people in this country have done this work at a private house. That is not a reason against it — it is a reason to ask any supplier what they have built, and to see it.

The vehicles are still here. The house is what you see.

And afterwards

There can be other benefits too.

They are real, but they are not the reason. The reason is that the cars fit.

Secondary effects
  • The ground. Whatever the second car was occupying is available again. A front garden is usually thirty or forty square metres — the most visible, best-oriented ground on the property, and the part of it a visitor sees first.
  • The frontage. A house whose first impression is its own architecture, rather than two parked cars, looks like a different house.
  • The car itself. A car that is enclosed or below ground is not on the road and not on view. Weather stops mattering.
Where this stands

Ordinary engineering, in an unusual place.

Everything above can be done in Ireland today. What is unsettled is how rarely it has been.

Stacked and lowered parking was developed in cities where land ran out first — Tokyo, Munich, Zurich — and it has been ordinary engineering there for decades.

Irish plots are not getting bigger and cars are not getting fewer. The question has been the same for thirty years: where do I find room for another car? The useful question may be a different one — whether the room is already there.
