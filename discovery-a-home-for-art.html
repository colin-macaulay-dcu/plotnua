A Home for Art

Your home could be more than somewhere to hang art.

Three possibilities. One home. And you decide how far to go.

What if a room you rarely use could give local art somewhere to be seen?

The overlooked opportunity

You may already have something an artist needs.

An original artwork could become part of your everyday life. You might offer space that helps an artist reach a new audience, experience an artist’s work in your home before deciding whether to buy it, or discover a piece to own.

Making the work is rarely an artist’s hardest problem. What happens to it afterwards is: where it lives, and who ever sees it.

What you have Space.

A wall you walk past. A spare room. A dry corner nothing is in.

What they don’t have Space.

Finished work. Made, framed, and stacked against a studio wall.

Somewhere to keep it. Somewhere to hang it. Somewhere to photograph it. Somewhere it might eventually find a buyer.

Not all of those need the same thing from you.

01 Possibility one

Open your home to art

Offer space to store, display, exhibit or potentially help sell artwork.

Nobody has to come to your door. You can take part without a single visitor entering your home, and you can change your mind between one arrangement and the next.

How it works

Four ways in.

You decide how far it goes.

  1. Store

    Finished work kept safely in space you are not using.

    Nobody sees it. Somewhere dry, out of the way, and agreed in advance — which to an artist with a small studio is worth more than it sounds.

  2. Display

    Selected work hangs where you live, and you live with it.

    It can also be photographed there, and work seen in a real room reads differently from work on a studio wall. Still no visitors.

  3. Open

    You choose to let somebody in.

    One viewing, a few people, or an occasional short exhibition. The only rung that puts anybody on your doorstep, and the only one you can skip.

  4. Help sell

    You help the work find somebody.

    Share the artist’s link, offer the photographs, mention it to somebody who would want it. The artist stays the seller.

Less of your home and timeMore involvement

If you go further

More involvement. More possibilities.

The more involved you choose to become, the greater the potential return may be — and the more there is to agree.

You could be paid for providing the space. If a work sells, you and the artist could agree that you receive a share. Neither is a rate: every arrangement is settled between two people, in advance.

And afterwards

Who benefits?

  • The artist. Somewhere for the work to be, and somewhere it can be seen. Wall space is the scarcest thing in an artist’s year.
  • You. A room with something in it worth looking at, and possibly something for the space.
  • Whoever buys it. Work seen beside a sofa rather than under gallery lighting — the hardest thing to picture, and the easiest thing to judge.

Nothing has to happen twice. But the artist now knows your wall.

02 Possibility two

Live with art

Explore experiencing an artist’s finished work in your home by direct arrangement.

Three ways it can happen

What if the gallery came to your home?

Some artists and galleries may agree to let you experience an artwork in your home before purchasing it. Others may consider temporary displays by individual arrangement.

  • Try it at home

    You may be able to live with a chosen work for a few days before you decide.

    Some galleries and artists offer this on approval. It is a courtesy rather than a right, so ask — and ask who insures the work while it is with you.

  • A home viewing

    Some artists and galleries bring a selection of work to your home.

    You see it on your own walls, in your own light. Ask whether there is a charge, how much they bring, and whether you are under any obligation to buy.

  • Rental, where available

    A handful of Irish services rent original work by the month.

    Most are aimed at film, corporate and design clients, though at least one also rents to homes. Ask about the minimum term and who insures the work.

PlotNua does not currently arrange artwork loans, rentals or placements. Any arrangement would need to be agreed directly with the artist or gallery. Whether any of these is on offer is up to the artist or gallery.

If you come to love the work: the artist is the seller. They own the work, set the price, deal with the buyer and receive the payment.

Before anything arrives, the custody, insurance and money questions below apply here too.

03 Possibility three

Discover & own

Find, buy or commission original Irish art.

Why it matters

A blank wall is not a decorating problem.

Most people fill it with something from a homeware shop, chosen in an afternoon. That is not a failure of taste. It is what happens when the alternative feels like a world you need an introduction to.

There is another option, and it is more ordinary than it sounds: an original work by an artist living and working within an hour of your house.

Cost

Does it have to be expensive?

No, and this assumption stops most people. Graduate shows, print editions, smaller works and emerging artists all sit well below what people expect.

PlotNua does not publish price guides. What a work costs depends on the artist, the medium and the size.

Where to look

Four routes to original art.

None of them require an introduction.

Route 1

Open studios and Culture Night

Artists open their working spaces and you can buy on the spot. Culture Night runs nationwide every September, free, coordinated by the Arts Council with local authorities.

Route 2

Galleries and artist-run spaces

A gallery represents its artists and handles the sale. Visual Artists Ireland describes a 50/50 split as standard, though it varies.

Route 3

Directly from the artist

Many artists sell from their own website or studio. The full price goes to the person who made the work.

Route 4

Fairs and annual exhibitions

Art Source fills the RDS each November. The RHA Annual Exhibition runs free from late spring through the summer, with almost everything for sale. The RDS Visual Art Awards exhibition opens each November, showing emerging artists chosen from graduate shows.

A note on tax

Buying locally keeps the money in the room. Under Revenue’s Artists’ Exemption, an artist’s income from original and creative work, including paintings and sculpture, can be exempt from income tax up to €50,000 a year, but only where Revenue has made a determination for that artist’s work. It does not cover USC or PRSI.

More on buying and commissioning
What you are actually buying

Original, print or limited edition.

Three words, used loosely. The difference decides the price and what you own.

  • An original is the work itself — one painting, one drawing, one sculpture. There is no second copy.
  • A limited edition is a set of identical prints from the same plate, screen or file, produced in a stated number and then stopped, usually signed and numbered.
  • An open edition has no limit. More can always be printed, which is why it costs less.
Commissioning

Asking for something made for your wall.

A commission means the artist makes a new work for a particular space — the answer when you like an artist but not the work currently available.

  • Not every artist takes commissions, and there is no obligation to. Ask early rather than assuming.
  • Expect a deposit and a timeline. Commissions commonly involve a deposit up front, an agreed size and subject, and a lead time in months rather than weeks.
  • Agree what approval means before work begins — whether you see it in progress, and what happens if the finished work is not what you pictured.
Getting it right

Most regrets are size regrets.

Not taste. Scale. A work that holds a gallery wall can look stranded above a sofa.

  • Size it against the furniture. Over a sofa or fireplace, aim for roughly two-thirds the width of the thing beneath it. Mark it on the wall in masking tape before you commit.
  • Hang it lower than you think. The centre should sit at about eye level, roughly 1.5m from the floor. Most people hang too high.
  • Look at the light. Direct sunlight fades works on paper and damages varnish. The wall opposite a south-facing window is the one to avoid.
Before you buy

Four things worth settling in writing.
  • Whether the frame is included, and who pays for it if it is not.
  • Delivery and installation. A large or heavy work needs proper fixings and sometimes a professional. Ask who normally does it.
  • Insurance. Household policies often cap individual items. For a higher-value work, tell your insurer rather than assume.
  • Copyright stays with the artist. Buying the work does not buy the right to reproduce it. Under the Copyright and Related Rights Act 2000 that right transfers only in writing, commissions included. Hanging it at home needs no permission. Exhibition sits outside the artist’s reproduction rights. Printing it on something does not.
Before anything arrives

Three things to settle first.

Custody

Agree what is coming, and who is covering it.

  • What work
  • How many pieces
  • What each is worth
  • The condition it arrives in

Do not assume a home policy protects somebody else’s artwork.

Your home

Tell your insurer what you intend to do.

The more routine or commercial the activity becomes, the more important it is to check. A Section 5 declaration gives a formal answer for €80, decided within four weeks.

Money

If money changes hands, it is income.

Rent-a-Room Relief does not apply — that scheme is for letting a room as living accommodation. The artist remains the seller and takes the buyer’s payment; anything you receive is a separate agreed amount, and income in the normal way.

In detail

What to agree with the artist
The space.
Which wall, which room, which corner — and what is not included. Mark it out before anything arrives.
Access and duration.
When the artist can come, how they get in, and how long the arrangement runs. A season is a natural unit. Agree the notice either of you gives, and what happens to work still in the house when it ends.
An inventory, with values.
What is coming, how many pieces, what each is worth, and the condition each arrives in. Photograph them on the day. This is the single most useful thing you can do, and it takes twenty minutes.
Photographs.
Whether work may be photographed in your home, whether your rooms may appear in those images, and where the artist may use them.
Fee or share.
Whether anything is being paid for the space, and whether you receive a share if a piece sells as a result of your involvement. Agree it in advance and write it down — one page, signed by both, costs nothing and prevents most disagreements.
Responsibility.
Who insures the work, and what happens if something is damaged.
In detail

Insurance, custody and your home

Do not assume you are covered. Allianz lists visitors’ property as a standard benefit on its Irish home policy, covered to €2,000 — but only where contents cover is held, and only for loss or damage the policy already covers. That is a benefit for a visitor’s belongings. It is not storage cover for somebody’s finished work, and it should not be read as one.

The artist’s cover does not close the gap either. Visual Artists Ireland advises artists to hold their own public liability insurance for an exhibition even where the venue is insured — because if a visitor is injured by an artwork, that can be the artist’s responsibility rather than the venue’s. It also notes that most such policies do not cover work in transit. Public liability is about harm to people, not damage to the work.

Tell your own insurer first. Bringing somebody else’s property into your home, for money, is exactly the kind of change an insurer expects to hear about. Ask before anything arrives, not after.

In detail

Money, selling and the planning line

The artist is the seller. Anything you are paid is a separate agreement between you and the artist. Keeping it that way is simpler for everybody, and it is the arrangement this page describes.

What you receive is income. A fee for the space, or a share when something sells, is income and is taxable in the normal way. Rent-a-Room Relief does not apply — Revenue’s scheme covers letting a room as residential accommodation, with a minimum letting period, and not for commercial purposes. There is no tax-free allowance here. If your net non-PAYE income for the year reaches €5,000, or your gross non-PAYE income reaches €30,000, Revenue treats you as a chargeable person and a different return applies.

Where the planning line sits. Planning permission is needed only where a change of use is material, and whether a change is material depends on the facts. Keeping a few pieces is a long way from running a sales business out of a dwelling. The more regular or commercial the activity becomes, the more important it is to check — and a Section 5 declaration is how you get a formal answer rather than an opinion. Nothing here is legal or tax advice, and the judgement is your local authority’s, not ours.

One thing worth knowing about the artist’s side. Under Revenue’s Artists’ Exemption, income from the sale of original artistic work — paintings and sculpture among the qualifying categories — can be exempt from Income Tax up to €50,000 a year, but only where Revenue has made a determination for that artist’s work. It does not cover USC or PRSI. It is the artist’s relief, never the host’s.

Frequently asked questions

Common questions.

Can artwork be moved or rotated later?

Yes. Rotating works you already own between rooms costs nothing. Renting by the month is covered under Live with art, above.

Can I spread the cost?

Sometimes, by arrangement with the gallery or artist. There is no state-backed instalment scheme in the Republic of Ireland. The Own Art interest-free scheme operates in Britain and Northern Ireland only.

How do I know the price is fair?

Compare the artist’s other work at similar sizes, and ask what the price includes. It generally follows size, medium and how established the artist is. PlotNua does not value or authenticate work.
