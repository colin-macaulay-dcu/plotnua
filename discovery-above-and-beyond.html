Above and Beyond

Imagine if your garden had a second level.

The Revelation

Look up

Stand at the back door and look at your garden. You are almost certainly measuring the ground.

Everyone thinks about garden space as a footprint — so much lawn, so much patio. A new room has to come out of it, so building something means the garden gets smaller.

Now look up.

The space above the lawn is already yours, and it rarely gets counted. What if the extra space was above it?

People build up there, in Ireland, in ordinary gardens, including small ones. You can buy one this week. And the thing that decides what you end up with is not the thing you would expect.

The Surprise

You don’t need a tree

A treehouse without a tree sounds like a contradiction, and it isn’t. A structure can bring its own support — posts, stilts, columns or an engineered frame — and stand up entirely on its own.

Irish and British builders describe the category that way themselves, and Irish suppliers sell it by name — a playhouse on stilts, with a ladder, a swing, and decking under. The decking under is the part worth noticing.

The moment a suitable tree stops being a requirement, the idea opens up to gardens where a traditional treehouse would not work.

There are three ways one of these can stand up.

01

Freestanding

The structure stands on its own supports, so no suitable tree is required.

02

Part tree, part support

A tree forms part of the structure, with additional engineered support carrying the rest. Common where there is a good tree, but not one that can do the whole job alone.

03

Tree-supported

The traditional version, where suitable trees form part of the support. It depends entirely on having the right tree in the right place.

Which route works depends on the garden, the structure and what you want to use it for. The important point is that a suitable tree is not a requirement.

The Range

What it can become

This is where people underestimate it. The picture in most heads is a small timber box for small children, outgrown in about three summers.

It does not have to be that. Laid end to end, the published work runs from a den a metre off the lawn to something an architect signs.

A raised den

A platform, a ladder or steps, a small enclosed space and a veranda. This is the volume product, and it is where most Irish gardens start.

A play and adventure structure

Towers, slides, monkey bars, climbing walls, nets, crow’s nests. Irish makers publish bridges between towers, drop bridges and in-ground trampolines at this level, not only at the ambitious end.

A substantial elevated room

Enclosed, boarded, glazed. One Irish maker’s published Dublin project is a games room treehouse, elevated and built around a mature birch to give a sense of being in the canopy while avoiding overlook of neighbouring gardens.

An adult retreat

The same maker publishes a County Louth retreat in a mature oak with a wrap-around deck that lets you follow the sun, and says that depending on the comfort level required these can be fully insulated and heated for year-round use.

A games room, studio or workspace

At the frontier these are published as standing product lines — studios, home offices, garden rooms in the air. In Ireland the games room is the evidenced example.

An architectural treehouse

The most ambitious published example built in Ireland is on the Atlantic coast: a look-out deck, a rope bridge, a reading nook, cedar cladding and a glass window in the floor, designed for a windy, exposed site. It was built by a British practice, and whether that practice currently takes on private commissions here is something PlotNua has not established.

One important distinction. This is not something anyone sells off a shelf. It is not a standard Irish product, it would not suit every garden, and nothing about a structure this size should be assumed to be exempted development.

Not every structure suits every use, and the use changes what has to be built. Insulation, power, glazing, how you get up there and how often you go — somewhere to read on a June evening and somewhere to work in February are different briefs.

The exemption limit

The exemption for a structure of this kind sets a limit of 3 metres, measured overall from ground level to the highest point, and allows 4 metres only where the roof is a tiled or slated pitched roof. The two conditions hold together: a felted or shingled roof at any pitch is on the 3 metre line, which is why quoting the higher figure on its own would be the most misleading thing this page could do.

What Irish suppliers actually publish

Timbertrove publish the only complete measured elevation we found in Ireland — a deck at 1.2 metres, 2.7 metres overall, felted roof — for the Hide Out Treehouse at €2,339. Geppetto Land and Garden Sheds Dublin each publish deck heights of about 4 feet for products in their ranges. No Irish catalogue product we found is published with a tiled or slated pitched roof, so in practice the ready-made market is working to the 3 metre figure.

The two classes

Class 3 covers garden structures of this kind and counts every one in the curtilage together. Since July 2026 a separate class has allowed a small detached house in a back garden without planning permission, for a limited period, where it is occupied along with the main house. They are different exemptions, and they share an overall area ceiling.

The arithmetic, and whose it is

The combined ceiling across both classes is 45 square metres. A detached garden house has a floor it cannot go below of 32 square metres. Taken together that leaves about 13 square metres — roughly a three-by-four-metre building plus a modest deck — for garden structures of this kind before the garden house route closes. Questions

The practical questions

Do I really not need a tree?
Correct, and builders in three countries say so in their own words. Irish makers describe the category as a raised playhouse and offer freestanding as well as tree-supported routes; Irish suppliers sell playhouses on stilts by name. Where there is no suitable tree, the structure brings its own support.

What does it actually cost?
Seven Irish suppliers publish prices, from €700 for a small flat-packed tree house from Garden Sheds Dublin up to €4,100 for the dearest activity centre from Geppetto Land. For a one-off designed for your garden, the designer at Forest Wild gives starting-budget guidance of €30,000 and up for a basic structure, and €60,000 and up for something more elaborate. That is his figure for the project, and he designs rather than builds. Between those two points nothing is published, and PlotNua will not borrow a price from another market to fill the space.

How high can I actually go?
Three metres overall, in most real cases. The regulations allow 4 metres only where the pitched roof is tiled or slated; everything else is capped at 3 metres. Since Irish makers typically finish these roofs in felt, shingles or steel, three is usually the number that applies. One Irish product publishes its full height at 2.7 metres, which is how we know the market builds to it.

Is it just for kids?
No. Irish makers publish a games room in a Dublin garden and an adult retreat in a County Louth oak with a wrap-around deck, and say these can be insulated and heated for year-round use. At the frontier, studios and home offices are standing product lines.

Can I sleep in it, or let someone live in it?
No. The garden-structure exemption excludes use for human habitation, and so does the Building Regulations exemption for a single storey building ancillary to a dwelling. This is a space to use, not a place to live.

Could I work in it?
Carefully. The Building Regulations exemption covers use that is exclusively recreational or for storage and expressly excludes use for a trade or business. Whether working from home for an employer falls inside that is not something PlotNua could establish. Ask before you build if that is the plan.

Will I lose garden space?
Some. Supports, access, foundations and the clear space around the structure all take ground. What you keep is much of the space underneath and around it — and at least one Irish supplier sells a model with usable decking under the platform.

Will the neighbours see in?
It is a real consideration and Irish makers design around it: one published Dublin project is elevated specifically to sit in the canopy while avoiding overlook of neighbouring gardens. Class 3 itself contains no window-to-boundary condition, so this is a design conversation rather than a rule, and for anything ambitious a planning one.

What if I do want to use a tree?
Have it looked at by an arborist before anything is designed. Separately, a planning authority can make a Tree Preservation Order under section 205 of the Planning and Development Act 2000, which can prohibit cutting down, topping, lopping or wilful destruction of the trees it names. Whether a TPO also restricts attaching a structure to a tree is not something PlotNua could establish.

Does a treehouse need planning permission?
That question has no general answer, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. The regulations do not mention treehouses at all, so whether a particular structure is exempt depends on its height, its area, where it sits, what else is in the garden and the site itself. The only binding answer is a Section 5 Declaration from your local authority.

Height, and where the three metre figure comes from

The limit, the condition attached to it, and the regulation in its own words.

The height limit, and why it is usually three metres

The planning rule
  • The 4-metre allowance in Class 3 is conditional on a tiled or slated pitched roof.
  • Any other covering falls under any other case and the limit is 3 metres.
What Irish suppliers publish
  • The coverings Irish makers publish are mineral felt, shingles and steel sheeting, including a slate-effect steel.
  • A slate-effect finish is not slate.
Building Regulations are a separate regime

A separate regime, worded differently. The Building Regulations are a separate regime from planning, administered by your local Building Control Authority rather than the planning section, and the two area figures are not the same.

  • Building Regulations 1997, S.I. No. 497 of 1997, Article 8 and the Third Schedule exempt a single storey building ancillary to a dwelling: detached, not more than 25 square metres, used exclusively for recreational or storage purposes and not for any trade or business, and no human habitation.
  • Height limit 3 metres, or — for a building with a pitched roof, and unlike the planning condition, with no requirement that it be tiled or slated — 4 metres.
  • The two regimes also use different area figures: planning now allows 30 square metres, the Building Regulations exemption is 25 square metres.
What this means

So the binding constraint on height is planning, not building control.

What the planning rules actually say — read directly

Class 3, as substituted by S.I. No. 338 of 2026, in force 27 July 2026. It permits the construction, erection or placing within the curtilage of a house of any tent, awning, shade or other object, greenhouse, garage, store, shed or other similar structure, subject to six conditions:

  • nothing forward of the front wall of the house;
  • a combined floor area for all such structures of no more than 30 square metres;
  • no reduction of private open space to the rear or side below 25 square metres;
  • external finishes, and the roof covering where the roof is tiled or slated, to conform with those of the house for anything placed to the side;
  • a height limit of 4 metres for a structure with a tiled or slated pitched roof and 3 metres in any other case;
  • and no human habitation, and no use for any purpose other than one incidental to the enjoyment of the house.
The area figure changed

The previous limit was 25 square metres; the 2026 substitution raised it to 30. Both are stated here because older guidance carries the old one.

What Class 3 does not say

Class 3 does not name treehouses, playhouses, play towers or elevated structures. Whether a raised structure is read that way is not something PlotNua could establish.

Source and date

Read first-party on the Irish Statute Book, 5 October 2026.

Space: what counts, and what has to be left

Why the limit is about the whole garden rather than one structure, and where the two classes meet.

It all adds up, across the whole garden

Class 3 counts every structure of this kind in the curtilage together, not one at a time. The combined total must not exceed 30 square metres. The previous limit was 25 square metres and a lot of guidance still says so, which is worth knowing if you are reading something older.

You have to leave open space behind

The same class says the structure must not reduce the private open space to the rear or side of the house below 25 square metres. On a small plot this bites before the area limit does.

Where it sits changes the specification

Nothing of this kind may go forward of the front wall of the house. And anything placed to the side of the house must have external finishes — and, where it has a tiled or slated roof, a roof covering — that conform with those of the house. A side-garden structure is not simply a rear-garden one moved over.

The detached garden house, and how it interacts

What the class permits

A separate class inserted in July 2026.

  • It permits a detached house in the rear garden of a principal house, for a period ending 31 December 2030, occupied only in conjunction with the main dwelling and not sold or subdivided separately.
  • Building control legislation including fire safety applies.
  • A notification must reach the planning authority not less than fourteen days before works commence.
  • It requires a floor area of not less than 32 square metres.
  • It caps that area, taken together with any structures previously placed under Class 3 in the same curtilage, at 45 square metres.

The consequence set out in this page is PlotNua’s reading of those two provisions together, not guidance from any authority. Build more than about thirteen square metres of garden structures and what remains of the 45 falls below the 32-square-metre floor.

The Class 3A arithmetic in full, and whose reading it is

What the separate route allows

Since July 2026 there is a separate route, a different class from the one that covers garden structures, and it comes with its own conditions.

  • It lets you put a whole small detached house in your back garden without planning permission, for a limited period, if it is occupied in conjunction with the main house.
  • Building regulations including fire safety apply.
  • You have to notify the council fourteen days before you start.
The catch: two numbers that interact
  • That route requires the house to be at least 32 square metres.
  • And it caps the house, taken together with the garden structures already built under the garden-structure class, at 45 square metres combined.

So the two interact. The more garden structure you build now, the less garden house is available later. Past roughly thirteen square metres of structures, what remains falls below the 32-square-metre floor and that route closes. This is PlotNua’s reading of two sets of regulations, not guidance from any authority. The arithmetic is ours.

For scale
  • A small treehouse deck is around four square metres.
  • A three-by-four-metre structure is twelve.

So you can usually have both — but only if somebody counts, and nobody is going to count for you at the point of sale. If a garden house is anywhere in your thinking, it is worth putting to your local authority before you order a treehouse, not after.

Trees, safety and insurance

The three questions that are somebody else’s expertise, and what to ask them.

What to get from whoever builds it

Six things worth getting before you pay anybody. Public liability and professional indemnity certificates. The written structural warranty, and what it excludes. Who owns the design you paid for — at least one builder keeps it. Who services it, how often, and at what rate. The payment schedule. And whether a Section 5 Declaration is being sought, and by whom.

What the builders themselves say about trees

The builders say it more plainly than we could.

  • A Longford maker describes the category as a raised playhouse, often called a tree house — on a raised platform, with a porch, climbing ropes, a ladder, steps and slides. No tree in the description at all.
  • A Clare maker put it as supported by one or more trees or freestanding amongst the branches — published material PlotNua read first-party and can no longer verify as current.
  • In the United Kingdom, one builder answers the question with a flat no you don’t and says it has already built many without trees.
  • Another UK builder heads a whole page you don’t need a tree to have a treehouse.
  • In Germany, an architecture practice has kept stilt houses as a standing category for twenty years.
  • The Irish market sells the thing by name: one supplier lists a playhouse on stilts, another one on stilts with a ladder, a swing, and decking under.

All read first-party.

The tree is a specialist question, and not the one you expect

How builders attach to a tree — two published methods
  • One builder guarantees never to damage the tree, because the structure is not attached to it at all.
  • The standard alternative uses an engineered bolt that lets the tree keep moving and growing.
  • Either way, branch removal goes to a professional tree surgeon.
  • One frontier builder 3D-scans the tree so the design is drawn to its actual geometry.
The counter-intuitive part: the risk is in the ground

For a freestanding structure near a tree, the arboricultural risk sits mostly in the footings, the excavation and where the materials get stacked, because all of that happens inside the root protection area. Your instinct will be to worry about bolts. The standard worries about the ground. That is PlotNua’s reading of the professional guidance, which is a British Standard rather than an Irish legal requirement.

Tree Preservation Orders

A planning authority can make a Tree Preservation Order under section 205 of the Planning and Development Act 2000, which can prohibit the cutting down, topping, lopping or wilful destruction of the trees it names. Contravening one, or even a proposed one, is an offence.

Attaching something is not in that list, but an individual order can carry its own conditions, so the order has to be read. Whether a TPO also restricts attaching a structure to a tree is not something PlotNua could establish.

Getting up there takes ground, and so does the space around it

Access is a design problem, not an afterthought. Steps, a ladder, a bridge or some combination, and all of them take up ground.

The only published figures for clear space
  • A cushioned surface extending at least 6 feet past the equipment.
  • Loose fill 12 inches deep for equipment up to 8 feet high.

That is one Irish supplier’s own practice, published as fall-zone guidance for its own range, expressed in feet and inches and evidently drawn from public-playground guidance. It is not an Irish requirement and nothing here presents it as one. It is still the most concrete answer anyone publishes to how much room you actually need around the thing.

What nobody should tell you

Nobody should tell you an elevated structure costs you no garden at all. Supports, access, foundations and the clear space around it all take some. The more accurate claim is that much of the garden underneath and around it can stay useful — and one Irish supplier sells exactly that, a playhouse on stilts with decking underneath.

Safety standards are a commitment, not a requirement

EN 1176, the European playground equipment standard, covers permanently installed equipment in public and shared settings — parks, schools, housing estates. Equipment intended for private family use at a single home falls outside its scope, so it is not a mandatory domestic requirement, and nothing here is presented as certified to it. If a builder tells you a structure meets a European safety standard, that is a commitment they have chosen to make. It is a good question to ask and a good answer to get.

Ask about the builder’s insurance, not just your own

Your own policy
  • The CCPC notes that buildings cover usually includes outbuildings such as a garden shed.
  • You may need to increase your cover if you extend or improve your home.
  • The CCPC says specific exclusions vary between policies and should always be checked.
  • The usual advice is to tell your insurer before you build, and that still holds.
The sharper question: the person building it
  • The CCPC says damage caused by someone working in your home is not usually covered by your own buildings policy.
  • You can only claim if the person who caused it has their own public liability insurance.
  • The CCPC says, twice, to check that anyone working on your home has it. So ask for the certificates and keep them.

For scale, one frontier builder publishes employers’ liability, public and products liability and professional indemnity cover, and offers copies on request.

PlotNua could not establish any general Irish position on elevated garden structures specifically, so treat all of this as the question to ask rather than the answer.

Trees, standards and insurance

Tree Preservation Orders
  • Section 205, Planning and Development Act 2000. A planning authority may make a Tree Preservation Order in the interests of amenity or the environment; it is a reserved function of the elected members.
  • An order may prohibit the cutting down, topping, lopping or wilful destruction of the trees it names, and contravening an order, or a proposed order pending decision, is an offence.
  • Attachment is not among the prohibitions the Act names, but an order may carry its own conditions.
  • Whether a TPO also restricts attaching a structure to a tree is not something PlotNua could establish.
Root protection

The professional framework for trees near construction is a British Standard rather than an Irish legal requirement. It calculates a root protection area at design stage, within which ground disturbance, excavation, trenching, utility installation and material storage should not occur. PlotNua’s inference that footings matter more than fixings for a freestanding structure follows from that, and is labelled as inference.

EN 1176
  • EN 1176 is the European standard for playground equipment.
  • Published scope descriptions state that it covers permanently installed public playground equipment and excludes items defined as toys in EN 71.
  • Equipment intended for private family use at a single home falls outside it.
  • The standard text itself sits behind a paywall and was not read; this is a catalogue scope statement, not a reading of the standard.
  • It is not a mandatory domestic requirement and nothing on this page is presented as certified to it.
Insurance

The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission, the statutory Irish consumer body, states:

  • that buildings cover usually includes garages or outbuildings such as a garden shed;
  • that if you extend or improve your home you may need to increase your buildings cover;
  • that standard exclusions are in every policy but specific exclusions vary and should always be checked;
  • that damage caused by someone working in your home is not usually covered and can only be claimed where that person has their own public liability insurance;
  • and, as a tip, to always check that anyone working on your home has it.

Getting a certain answer, and the one Irish case

How to turn an interpretation into a decision, and the single Irish precedent we found.

Exemption can disappear for reasons that have nothing to do with size

Article 9 of the same regulations removes exempted-development status in a list of circumstances: contravening a condition of an existing permission, interfering with a view or prospect of special amenity value protected by the development plan, obstructing a public right of way, works in an architectural conservation area that would materially affect its character, or extending an unauthorised structure. Class 3 is also named specifically: in an area covered by a special amenity area order it is not exempted development at all. If the house is a protected structure, or in a conservation area, the route is a Section 57 Declaration rather than a Section 5.

The way to get an actual answer

Under section 5 of the Planning and Development Act 2000 any person can ask their planning authority for a declaration on whether something is development, and whether it is exempted development.

  • The authority must issue the declaration and the main reasons for it within four weeks, or within three weeks of receiving any further information it asks for.
  • The fee in the county whose register we read is €80, payable to the planning authority.
  • The application needs the relevant site maps.
Two things worth knowing before you apply
  • A Section 5 considers only whether something is development and whether it is exempt — no planning policy, no design view.
  • The question has to be posed clearly or the application comes back invalid, which is the most common way people lose a month.

Getting a binding answer, and one real case

The statutory route

Under section 5 of the Planning and Development Act 2000 any person may, on payment of the prescribed fee, request a declaration from the planning authority on whether something is or is not development, and is or is not exempted development.

  • The authority must issue the declaration and the main reasons and considerations within four weeks, or within three weeks of receiving requested further information.
  • A declaration may be referred for review within four weeks of issue.
  • Details of every declaration are entered in a public register, which is why the case below is readable.
  • The fee in the register we read is €80, set by that planning authority, and the application requires the relevant site maps.
  • A Section 5 considers only development and exemption; no planning policy is considered.
  • An unclear question is returned invalid.
The case

Louth County Council’s 2025 register records a request asking whether a recently erected playroom structure in the rear of a property was development and was exempted development. The decision was a refusal. The register gives no reasons, no dimensions, and does not say whether the structure was elevated. It is one declaration by one authority and it is not a rule that binds others. Read first-party, 5 October 2026.

One real Irish case, and what it does not tell you

In 2025 a homeowner in County Louth asked the council whether a recently erected playroom structure in the rear of a property was development, and whether it was exempted development. The council refused the declaration. Read that carefully, because it is thinner than it looks. The public register records the question and the decision but not the reasons, and it says nothing about the structure’s dimensions or whether it was elevated at all. It is one case, decided by one authority, and it is not a rule that binds anybody else. What it does show is that this question reaches councils, and that already having built the thing does not settle it.

Slope, access, light and overlooking

The site conditions that decide more than the regulations do.

Slope and access decide more than they should

One supplier’s floor height varies with the slope of the garden. Another supplies levelling blocks on the condition that the site is clear and level. A third publishes a site and access requirements page, because getting the materials down the side of a house is the classic lost day. Re-levelling is itself a planning question. In one published Louth case a proposal to raise and lower rear-garden ground levels by no more than a metre was refused exempted-development status.

Light and overlooking are design questions, not rules

Class 3 itself contains no window-to-boundary condition, so this is not something the regulations decide for you. It is still real. One Irish maker’s published Dublin project was deliberately elevated to sit in the canopy while avoiding overlook of neighbouring gardens. Another tells customers plainly that the bigger tree houses would not suit a smaller garden because they may block views and a lot of sunlight. That is a supplier talking someone out of the larger model, which is worth more than a brochure.
