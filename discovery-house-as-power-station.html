The Discovery

Could a house help power AI?

A house has always been somewhere electricity arrives. Computing is beginning to move the other way.

Emerging models are beginning to spread computing across many smaller sites — and some of those sites are ordinary homes. A property could contribute electricity, battery capacity, connectivity, secure space, or even make use of the heat that computing produces.

None of this is available to an Irish homeowner today. What follows is what is being built elsewhere.

Already being built
  • Britain A boiler-sized unit holds 450–500 compute modules, and the heat they produce warms the household’s water. Thermify’s HeatHub. The company pays for the electricity and sells the computing. Deployed through the SHIELD trial with UK Power Networks.
  • United States Sixteen NVIDIA GPUs installed at a home beside a smart panel and battery, using power the house is not consuming. SPAN’s XFRA, backed by NVIDIA and the homebuilder PulteGroup, rolling out from Q3 2026. Households are not paid cash: they receive the equipment, the installation, and discounted electricity and internet.
  • United States Home solar and batteries supplying capacity into the programmes that serve AI data centres. Sunrun with Voltus, announced August 2026, in the PJM and MISO grid regions. Participating households are compensated.
  • Europe One European platform describes household nodes as a layer of its network: homes with rooftop solar and batteries becoming compute sites. CYANIS AI, Hamburg. Its specified household unit is 500W and desktop-sized, behind the meter. The architecture is published; the household layer is not an open programme.
What might matter

If a home were ever part of a network like this, these are the pieces the emerging models keep naming.

What would matter will depend on the technology and the provider. For now, these are simply the features appearing again and again in the models being developed.

  • 500WThe household unit CYANIS specifies: desktop form factor, silent, behind the meter.
  • 450–500Compute modules inside a single Thermify HeatHub, heating a household’s water.
The pieces
  • Energy. The ability to generate or provide electricity.
  • Storage. Battery capacity that can shift when electricity is used.
  • Connectivity. A reliable high-speed connection for computing equipment.
  • Space. A secure physical place to put it.
  • Heat. Some models make use of the heat computing produces.

Not here yet. But some of the pieces already are.

Today in Ireland

The first pieces are already here.

More than 140,000 Irish households are already paid for the electricity their roofs produce. This is not a trial.

  • Make The roof turns daylight into electricity the house uses straight away.
  • Time A smart meter lets some hours of the day cost far less than others.
  • Store A battery holds the afternoon’s electricity until the evening.
  • Sell What the house does not use goes back out, and you are paid for it.
  • Grant Up to €1,800 towards panels, and no VAT on them. The house must have been built and lived in before 2021, and it can be claimed once at any address. It shrinks each year and finishes in 2029.
  • Export The first €400 a year is free of income tax, to the end of 2028. Every supplier must pay you for what you send back, though how much is up to them. You need a smart meter — four in five Irish homes now have one.
  • Permission No planning permission for panels on a house, and no limit on the area. Roof limits still apply near a small number of airports and helipads. There is no grant for a home battery.

Grant, tax and permission in detail
The grant. Up to €1,800 towards panels, and no VAT to pay on them. The house has to have been built and lived in before 2021, and the grant can only be claimed once at any address — if a previous owner claimed it, it has already been used. It gets smaller each year and finishes in 2029, so waiting costs you some of it. Your installer applies to ESB Networks before anything goes on the roof, and that takes a few weeks; afterwards the house needs a new BER assessment before the grant is paid.

Tax. The first €400 a year you earn from exporting is free of income tax, up to the end of 2028. Above that it is taxed like any other income. You do not need to declare the exempt amount at all.

Permission. Panels on the roof of a house are exempt, with no limit on area. The only exception is a small number of areas near airports and helipads, where roof limits still apply because of glare. Most homes are nowhere near one.

Who stands behind the work. SEAI’s own words: it “does not approve, guarantee, or warranty a company or their works.” The State pays part of the cost. It does not stand behind whoever does the job. Choosing the installer is still entirely yours.

What it changes day to day
Less than people expect, and that is the point. The kettle still boils. The heating works the way it always did. Nothing in the house has to be used differently.

What changes is quieter. The immersion comes on at three in the morning instead of six in the evening. The car charges while everybody sleeps. On a bright Saturday the house runs itself for a few hours and the meter turns the other way.

If you have an immersion, a dishwasher or a car in the driveway, moving them to the cheap hours costs nothing and changes the bill. Which hours are cheap, and how cheap, depends on your supplier. Once it is set up, there is very little to think about.

What is not possible in Ireland yet
Hosting computing. There is no Irish residential compute programme. None of the British, American or European models above is open to an Irish homeowner, and none of them transfers automatically: each depends on a provider, a grid arrangement and a market that would have to exist here first.

Using the car as a battery. An electric car holds far more electricity than a home battery, but powering your house from your car is not approved for Irish homes, and export from a car is not expected here for a few years.

Homes working together. Streets of houses with panels and batteries can be coordinated to behave like one small power station. It has been tested in Ireland. It is not yet something a homeowner can join.

PlotNua is watching all of it. We will say so plainly if and when any of it becomes something an Irish homeowner can actually do.

Your roof can already make electricity. Your battery can already store it. Your meter can already measure what leaves the house. What PlotNua is watching for is the model that connects those pieces to computing.
