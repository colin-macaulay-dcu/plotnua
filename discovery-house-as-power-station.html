The House as a Power Station

What if your home could help power the future?

Most houses only use electricity. A growing number of Irish homes now make some of their own, and are paid for what they do not use.

Why This Discovery?

Who is this for?

Somebody with a roof that gets the sun for most of the day. Somebody whose car charges in the driveway overnight. Somebody who had a new meter fitted and never thought about it again.

This Discovery is for anyone who wants to know what their own house could do — and what is worth doing now rather than later.

It is not about buying panels. It is about understanding what your property is already capable of.

The Opportunity

What does this actually mean?

Your house does one thing with electricity today. It buys it.

It could do four.

  • Make it. The roof turns daylight into electricity the house uses straight away.
  • Time it. With a smart meter, some hours of the day cost far less than others.
  • Sell it. Whatever the roof makes and the house does not use goes back out, and you are paid for it.
  • Store it. A battery holds the afternoon’s electricity until the evening.

The first three are easier to arrange than the last one, and that is the order we will take them in.

Why Now

Why are people talking about this now?

Not because the technology changed. Because the roof and the meter did.

The rules on the roof relaxed. You no longer need planning permission for solar panels on a house, and there is no limit on how much of the roof they cover. There is no VAT on them either.

The meter arrived. More than four in five Irish homes now have a smart meter, fitted by ESB Networks.

And the meter is the part that matters. Without one, nobody can measure what your house sends back, so nobody can pay you for it.

More than 140,000 households in Ireland are already being paid for the electricity their roofs produce. This is not a trial. It is running.

Today

What can an Irish home do today?

Make it

There is a grant of up to €1,800 towards panels, and no VAT to pay on them.

Not every house qualifies. It has to have been built and lived in before 2021. And the grant can only be claimed once at any address, so if a previous owner claimed it, it has already been used.

It gets smaller each year and finishes in 2029, so waiting costs you some of it.

Time it

A smart meter lets your supplier charge different prices at different hours. Night is cheaper than evening.

If you have an immersion, a dishwasher or a car in the driveway, moving them to the cheap hours costs nothing and changes the bill.

Which hours are cheap, and how cheap, depends on your supplier. It is worth checking what yours offers.

Sell it

On a bright day the roof usually makes more than the house is using. Whatever is left over goes back out to the grid.

Every electricity supplier in Ireland has to pay you for it. How much they pay is up to them, and it can change, so it is worth seeing what others offer.

The first €400 a year you earn this way is free of income tax, up to the end of 2028. Above that it is taxed like any other income. You do not need to declare the exempt amount at all.

Store it

A battery holds what the roof makes in the afternoon so the house can use it that evening.

There is no grant for a home battery. There is one for the panels, but not for storing what they make. Worth knowing, because it is easy to assume otherwise.

Day to Day

What does it change day to day?

Less than people expect, and that is the point.

The kettle still boils. The heating works the way it always did. Nothing in the house has to be used differently.

What changes is quieter. The immersion comes on at three in the morning instead of six in the evening. The car charges while everybody sleeps. On a bright Saturday the house runs itself for a few hours and the meter turns the other way.

Once it is set up, there is very little to think about. The app is interesting for a while, and then it is just there.

Before You Start

What should you think about before you start?

None of this is technical. It is all about your own property.

Which way the roof faces, and what shades it. A roof in shade for half the day makes less than the same roof in the open. This decides more than the equipment does.

Whether you have a smart meter. Without one, everything still works — you just will not be paid for what you send back.

Whether the grant has already been used on the house. It can only be claimed once at any address.

The age of the house. Built and lived in before 2021, or the grant does not apply.

How long it takes. Your installer applies to ESB Networks before anything goes on the roof, and that takes a few weeks. Afterwards the house needs a new BER assessment before the grant is paid.

If you live close to an airport. There are areas near airports and helipads where roof limits still apply, because of glare. Most homes are nowhere near one.

And one that is not about your property at all:

SEAI’s own words: it “does not approve, guarantee, or warranty a company or their works.”

The State pays part of the cost. It does not stand behind whoever does the job. Choosing the installer is still entirely yours.

PlotNua does not size systems, quote for equipment, or estimate what you would earn. Export rates are set by suppliers and change, so we do not print one.

Looking Ahead

Looking Ahead

Everything above can be arranged in Ireland today. Everything below cannot.

Close, but not here yet

Using the car as a battery. An electric car holds far more electricity than a home battery, so sending some of it back to the house is an obvious idea.

It is not available here. Powering your house from your car is not approved for Irish homes, and selling power from a car back to the grid is not expected here for a few years yet. If an installer tells you otherwise, ask them to show you the approval.

Homes working together. Streets of houses with panels and batteries can be coordinated to behave like one small power station. It has been tested in Ireland. It is not yet something a homeowner can join.

Beginning elsewhere

Three examples. None of them Irish, all of them real.

In Britain, a unit about the size of a boiler holds hundreds of small computers. They do paid work for other companies, and the heat that produces warms the household’s water.

In the United States, a company installs computing equipment at houses alongside a battery, using the electricity the house is not using. The owners are not paid in cash — they get the equipment and cheaper electricity instead.

Also in the United States, a solar company now lets the batteries in its customers’ homes help supply large data centres at busy times. Those households are paid for it.

Why this is happening

Computing uses a lot of electricity, and the amount is rising. New power stations take years to build. Houses already have roofs, connections and space.

It is too early to say whether any of it reaches Ireland. What is clear is that the things that would make a house ready for it — a working roof, a battery, a smart meter — are the same things worth having anyway.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions.

Do I need planning permission for solar panels?
No. Panels on the roof of a house are exempt, with no limit on area. The only exception is a small number of areas near airports and helipads.

Do I need a smart meter?
Not to have panels. Yes, to be paid for what you send back. More than four in five Irish homes already have one.

Will I actually be paid?
Yes. Every supplier has to pay you for it, and more than 140,000 households are already getting it. How much depends on who you are with.

Is that money taxed?
The first €400 a year is not, up to the end of 2028. Above that it is taxed as normal income.

Is there a grant for a battery?
No. There is one for panels, but not for batteries.

Can I charge my car from my panels?
Yes, if the sun is out while the car is plugged in. Sending electricity the other way — from the car back to the house or the grid — is not available in Ireland yet.
