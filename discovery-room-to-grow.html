Four Ways to Add a Room

The garden, the attic, the garage, or an extension.

The house filled up long before the property did.

Why This Discovery?

Where should the space come from?

Most people start by thinking about the house. The rest of the property is worth asking about too.

There are four ways to answer that, and most houses can do more than one of them. This Discovery is about choosing between them, before you start looking at anything.

Every one of them ends with the same thing: another room. What they cost you is different in each case — money, garden, attic, months of disruption, and how easily you could change your mind afterwards.

One property, four places another room could come from.

The Four Routes

What each one gives you.

The same seven questions, asked of all four.

Extend the house

A room that is part of the house, on the same heating and the same floor.

Disruption
The highest. A wall comes out and the room behind it is a building site.
Speed
The slowest of the four.
Cost
Roughly €2,000 to €3,500 per square metre.
Planning
Rarely an obstacle at ordinary sizes. More likely if you go up rather than out.
Adds to the house
Yes. It becomes part of the floor area.
Later on
Hard to undo, and rarely worth undoing.
Costs you
Garden, permanently.

Convert the attic

A room in space the house already has, with nothing added to the site.

Disruption
Contained. The stairs are the disruptive part.
Speed
Quicker than extending.
Cost
Roughly €18,000 to €55,000.
Planning
Rarely an obstacle inside. A dormer at the front is the exception.
Adds to the house
Usually, where the head height and the stairs work.
Later on
Permanent, and the storage does not come back.
Costs you
The attic.

Convert the garage

A room on the ground floor, already built, already roofed, already dry.

Disruption
Low. Most of it happens behind a door you can close.
Speed
Often the quickest of the four.
Cost
Roughly €10,000 to €30,000.
Planning
Rarely an obstacle for family use. Business use is the exception.
Adds to the house
Usually, though the garage does not come back.
Later on
Reversible in principle. Rarely reversed in practice.
Costs you
The garage, and wherever its contents were going to go.

Build in the garden

A separate room at the end of the garden, a short walk from the back door.

Disruption
The lowest. Most of the work happens outside the house.
Speed
Weeks rather than months.
Cost
Roughly €8,000 to €18,000 for an insulated room you can use all year.
Planning
Rarely an obstacle at ordinary sizes.
Adds to the house
It is a separate building, so it usually sits outside the floor area.
Later on
The easiest of the four to change your mind about.
Costs you
Garden.

The Garden Route

The one route where the rules changed.

Since 27 July 2026 you can build up to 30 m² in your back garden with no planning application at all. That is about 5 m by 6 m. The limit before that was 25 m².

Whether your own garden qualifies depends on its size, its shape and what is already built on it. The conditions are set out in the 2026 exempted development regulations.

Most of what people want the room for is already allowed — a home office, a studio, a gym, a playroom, a study, somewhere for the teenagers to go.

Prices, taken from garden rooms PlotNua has already researched and compared:

  • From about €3,600. A timber building, lightly insulated or not insulated at all. Comfortable in spring and autumn, cold in January. Gardenhouse24 Monaco 28 at €3,624.
  • About €8,000 to €18,000. Insulated walls, floor and roof, double glazing, and a finished interior you can work in all year. By far the largest group. Sprout Pod 6m at €10,250.
  • €18,000 and above. More floor area, more glazing, better heating and a higher internal finish. Concept Modular Cabin 25 m² at €49,750.

Usually extra: the base, delivery, installation, electricity, heating and any groundworks. Indicative prices reviewed in August 2026.

Planning is one part of it. Building Regulations and Building Control apply separately, fire safety depends on how close the room sits to the house and the boundary, and if your house is protected or inside an architectural conservation area the exemption can be restricted. None of that is a reason not to proceed. All of it is a reason to ring the council before you order.

The teenagers get their own space, and the family gets the house back.

Your Property

What decides it on your property?

  • What the house already has. A garage, or an attic with the height to stand up in, removes a whole route from the list or puts one on it.
  • How you get to the back. Side access makes building in the garden simpler. Everything has to come through the house without it.
  • How much garden you are willing to give up. Two of the four take some, and two of them take none.
  • Whether you can live in the house during the work. This decides more projects than cost does.
Room or Home

If somebody needs somewhere to live.

Everything above gives you a room. Nobody sleeps in it.

The moment somebody is going to live there — an adult child, a parent, a carer — it stops being a room and becomes a home, and a different set of rules applies. Those rules set a minimum size as well as a maximum, and the home stays part of your property: it cannot be sold or divided off.

Looking Ahead

Looking Ahead

Everything above can be done in Ireland today. What follows is less settled.

The garden rules are new

The 30 m² exemption came in on 27 July 2026. It has not yet been through a full year of local authority interpretation, and how strictly the conditions are read may vary from one council to the next until it has.

Three of the four routes are not priced yet

PlotNua holds published prices for garden rooms and has compared them. For extending, the attic and the garage, the figures on this page are what the work generally costs in Ireland rather than researched prices. That side of the research is being built now, and this page will keep saying so until it is done.

What does not change

Whatever happens to prices or to guidance, the four routes are decided by the property. An attic with head height, a garage, side access to the back, and how much garden you are willing to give up. None of that depends on a rule.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions.

Can we do more than one?
Yes, and plenty of houses end up doing two over several years. Choosing the first one well usually makes the second one easier.

Which one adds the most value?
That depends on the house and the area, and we would rather say so than make up a figure. As a rule the three that add floor area to the house behave differently from the one that adds a separate building.

What if none of them work?
Some houses genuinely have no attic height, no garage and no garden worth building on. Where that is the case, the property will tell you quickly, and it is better to know at the start than three months in.

How soon do we have to decide?
There is no clock on this. The decision is worth taking slowly, because it is the one that sets everything after it.

Do any of these need planning permission?
It depends on the route and the size. Extending and converting are rarely an obstacle at ordinary sizes — going up rather than out, a dormer at the front, or using a converted garage for a business are the usual exceptions. In the garden, up to 30 m² has needed no planning application since 27 July 2026, subject to conditions. None of that is a substitute for one call to your local authority about your own address.

How long does a garden room take?
A single room is one of the quicker home projects — often a matter of weeks, depending on the design and how easy the garden is to get into.

Can I work from a garden room?
Your own work, yes. Teaching, clients or a business may need permission depending on how much of it there is. Ring the council first.

Can someone sleep in it?
Not under these rules. A separate rule covers a one-bedroom home in the garden, with its own size and conditions — Build a Home in Your Garden.
